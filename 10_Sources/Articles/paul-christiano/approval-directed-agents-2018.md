# Approval-directed agents

## Citation

Christiano, Paul. "Approval-directed agents". AI Alignment Forum, 22 November 2018. https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/7Hr8t6xwuuxBTqADK/approval-directed-agents-1.

## One-line summary

Christiano proposes agents that choose the action their overseer would most approve of on reflection, rather than agents that maximise a goal, as a low-commitment route to safe behaviour.

## Key claims

- An approval-directed agent estimates the rating its overseer would give an action after considering it at length.
- The quality of its decisions is bounded by the lesser of the agent's and the overseer's ability.
- Approval-direction makes minimal commitments, so the agent responds to unforeseen situations as the overseer would most approve.
- An optimal agent can still take actions with consequences the overseer would endorse but could not anticipate.
- The framing avoids fixing a goal in advance, reducing the risk of misspecified objectives.

## Excerpts

> "Estimate the expected rating Hugh would give each action if he considered it at length."
> ~ Definition of the approval-directed decision rule

> "The quality of approval-directed decisions is limited by the minimum of Arthur's ability and Hugh's ability."
> ~ On the ability bound

> "Approval-direction involves only extremely minimal commitments. If an approval-directed AI encounters an unforeseen situation, it will respond in the way that we most approve of."
> ~ On robustness to novelty

> "An optimal agent may make decisions that have consequences Hugh would approve of, even if Hugh can't anticipate those consequences himself."
> ~ On outperforming the overseer

## Reveals about tendency of thought

- Christiano prefers agents bounded by overseer approval over goal-maximisers, reflecting his distrust of fixed objectives.
- The "Hugh and Arthur" device shows his use of concrete personas to reason about abstract agency.
- Approval-direction is the conceptual seed of iterated amplification, where the overseer is itself amplified.

# Using Massive Online Choice Experiments to Measure Changes in Well-being

## Citation

Brynjolfsson, Erik, Avinash Collis, and Felix Eggers. "Using Massive Online Choice Experiments to Measure Changes in Well-being". Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 116, no. 15 (2019): 7250-7255. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1815663116.

## One-line summary

The authors use incentive-compatible choice experiments to value free digital goods, showing that they create large welfare gains that GDP and productivity statistics miss.

## Key claims

- GDP measures production, not well-being, and is a flawed welfare metric.
- Consumer surplus is a superior, more direct measure of consumer well-being.
- Zero-price digital goods contribute nothing to GDP yet generate large welfare gains.
- The median user required about $48 in compensation to forgo Facebook for one month.
- Periodic online choice experiments can supplement the national accounts.

## Excerpts

> "Gross domestic product (GDP) and derived metrics such as productivity have been central to our understanding of economic progress and well-being. In principle, changes in consumer surplus provide a superior, and more direct, measure of changes in well-being, especially for digital goods."
> ~ Abstract

> "For example, the median user needed a compensation of about $48 to forgo Facebook for 1 mo."
> ~ Abstract

> "Our overall analyses reveal that digital goods have created large gains in well-being that are not reflected in conventional measures of GDP and productivity."
> ~ Abstract

> "While it is a good measure of production, GDP is a significantly flawed measure of well-being."
> ~ Significance / Introduction

## Reveals about tendency of thought

- The paper extends his measurement-first instinct from productivity to welfare itself.
- Valuing zero-price goods restates his recurring claim that official statistics understate the digital economy.
- The empirical, experiment-based method shows his preference for direct evidence over assumption.

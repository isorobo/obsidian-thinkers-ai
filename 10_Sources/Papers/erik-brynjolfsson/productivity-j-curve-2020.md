# The Productivity J-Curve: How Intangibles Complement General Purpose Technologies

## Citation

Brynjolfsson, Erik, Daniel Rock, and Chad Syverson. "The Productivity J-Curve: How Intangibles Complement General Purpose Technologies". National Bureau of Economic Research Working Paper No. 25148, October 2018, revised January 2020. https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148.

## One-line summary

A model of intangible complementary investment explains why general purpose technologies first depress and then lift measured productivity, producing a J-shaped error in total factor productivity statistics.

## Key claims

- General purpose technologies require large intangible investments in new processes, business models, and human capital.
- Because intangibles are poorly measured, productivity growth is underestimated early and overestimated later.
- The resulting error in measured total factor productivity follows a J-curve shape.
- The model explains Solow's productivity paradox as one instance of a general phenomenon.
- Adjusting for intangibles tied to hardware and software raises measured TFP to 15.9% above official figures by the end of 2017.
- AI-related intangible capital effects on measured productivity are small but growing.

## Excerpts

> "General purpose technologies (GPTs) such as AI enable and require significant complementary investments, including co-invention of new processes, products, business models and human capital."
> ~ Abstract

> "Our model generates a Productivity J-Curve that can explain the productivity slowdowns often accompanying the advent of GPTs, as well as the increase in productivity later."
> ~ Abstract

> "Our adjusted measure TFP is 11.3% higher than official measures at the end of 2004, and 15.9% higher than official measures at the end of 2017."
> ~ Abstract

> "As firms adopt a new GPT, total factor productivity growth will initially be underestimated because capital and labor are used to accumulate unmeasured intangible capital stocks."
> ~ Section 1 (Introduction)

## Reveals about tendency of thought

- The paper formalises Brynjolfsson's recurring claim that technology pays off only after organisational restructuring, the engine of his J-curve framing.
- Treating intangibles as the missing variable in productivity statistics reflects his measurement-first approach to macroeconomic puzzles.
- Reaching back to Solow's paradox shows that he reads current AI debates as a continuation of a decades-old pattern, not a break from it.

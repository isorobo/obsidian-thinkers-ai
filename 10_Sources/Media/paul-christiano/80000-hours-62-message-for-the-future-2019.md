# Paul Christiano on whether we should leave a helpful message for future civilisations

## Citation

Christiano, Paul, and Robert Wiblin. "Paul Christiano on whether we should leave a helpful message for future civilisations — just in case humanity dies out". 80,000 Hours Podcast, episode 62, 5 August 2019. https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/paul-christiano-a-message-for-the-future/.

## One-line summary

Christiano revisits his evolving alignment views and explores the long-shot idea of leaving a durable, intelligible message for any civilisation that might follow humanity.

## Key claims

- His earlier view demanded an AI that grasped exactly what humans want over the very long term.
- A durable message could shape the technological trajectory of a successor civilisation.
- Burial alone fails because geological time reshapes the earth's surface.
- A useful message would carry enough information to attempt genuine understanding.
- The exercise tests how robustly humanity could transmit values across deep time.

## Excerpts

> "I used to think of needing to hit this, like you really need to have an AI system that understands exactly what humans want over the very long term."
> ~ On how his alignment view changed

> "It could have a huge technological effect on the trajectory of development, and also reasonably likely have a reasonable effect."
> ~ On the impact of a message

> "You can't just bury a thing, because hundreds and millions of years is long enough that the surface of the earth is no longer the surface."
> ~ On the difficulty of durable storage

> "We would have put a lot of information into it really attempting to be understood."
> ~ On designing for intelligibility

## Reveals about tendency of thought

- Christiano updates his own positions openly, noting where his earlier alignment demands were too strong.
- The message-to-the-future puzzle shows his willingness to reason rigorously about low-probability, high-stakes scenarios.
- The concern with transmitting values across deep time connects to his broader worry about preserving human direction.
